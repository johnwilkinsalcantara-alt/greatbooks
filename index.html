I am a passionate and curious student who finds immense joy in discovering worlds through literature. As a reader, I gravitate toward narratives that challenge my understanding of human nature and the moral complexities that shape civilizations.

My literary interests span epic poetry, mythology, philosophical fiction, and historical narratives. I am particularly drawn to works that explore the tension between duty and desire, the hero's journey, and the timeless struggle between good and evil. Outside of class, I enjoy reading contemporary novels, creative non-fiction, and graphic novels that re-imagine classical stories for modern audiences.

Part II

Literary Analyses

A compilation of seven literary analyses accomplished throughout the course

1
Reader-Response Criticism

Voice Out of the Whirlwind (Job 38)

by Biblical / Traditional

The "Voice Out of the Whirlwind" in Job 38 is one of the most powerful passages in all of ancient literature. After chapters of anguished debate between Job and his friends about the nature of suffering and divine justice, God finally speaks — not with comforting answers, but with a torrent of rhetorical questions that challenge the very foundations of human understanding.

2
Moral-Philosophical Criticism

The Panchatantra

by Vishnu Sharma (attributed)

The Panchatantra, one of the oldest collections of interrelated animal fables in world literature, was originally composed in Sanskrit around the 3rd century BCE. Attributed to the scholar Vishnu Sharma, the text was designed as a practical manual for educating young princes in the art of governance, diplomacy, and wise living — all disguised within charming animal stories.

3
Archetypal / Mythological Criticism

The Mahabharata

by Vyasa (traditionally attributed)

The Mahabharata is not merely an epic — it is a civilization compressed into verse. With over 100,000 couplets, it is the longest poem ever written, and its scope encompasses war, philosophy, love, duty, and the very nature of dharma (cosmic law). At its heart is the devastating war between the Pandavas and the Kauravas — cousins whose rivalry destroys an entire generation.

4
Formalist / New Criticism

The Iliad

by Homer

Homer's Iliad, composed around the 8th century BCE, chronicles a few pivotal weeks during the tenth year of the Trojan War. Yet this narrow temporal focus belies the epic's enormous thematic ambition: it is, at its core, a meditation on rage, honor, mortality, and what it means to be human in a world governed by capricious gods.

5
Psychoanalytic Criticism

The Odyssey

by Homer

If the Iliad is a poem about rage and glory, the Odyssey is its psychological counterpart — a poem about longing, identity, and the perilous journey homeward. Odysseus' ten-year voyage from Troy to Ithaca is not merely a geographical odyssey but a profound exploration of the human psyche.

6
Historical Criticism

The Song of Roland

by Anonymous (Old French, c. 1100 CE)

The Song of Roland (La Chanson de Roland), composed around 1100 CE, is the earliest and most celebrated of the French chansons de geste ("songs of heroic deeds"). It dramatizes the historical Battle of Roncevaux Pass (778 CE), in which the rearguard of Charlemagne's army was ambushed — though the poem transforms a minor military disaster into an epic clash between Christendom and Islam.

7
Feminist Criticism

The Tale of Genji

by Murasaki Shikibu

Murasaki Shikibu's The Tale of Genji (c. 1000–1012 CE) is widely considered the world's first novel. Written by a woman of the Heian court, it chronicles the life, loves, and political entanglements of the "shining prince" Hikaru Genji. Yet beneath its elegant surface lies a penetrating examination of gender, power, and the limited agency available to women in aristocratic Japan.

Part III

My Favorite Literary Masterpiece

The literary work that resonated with me the most throughout this course

The Mahabharata

by Vyasa (traditionally attributed)

Overview

The Mahabharata is the longest epic poem in world literature, comprising over 100,000 couplets organized into 18 books (parvas). At its narrative core is the devastating war between two branches of a royal family — the virtuous Pandavas and the envious Kauravas — for control of the kingdom of Hastinapura. But the epic is far more than a war story: it encompasses philosophy (the Bhagavad Gita), law, ethics, cosmology, and countless sub-narratives that explore every facet of human experience.

Why This Is My Favorite

Among all the masterpieces studied in this course, the Mahabharata resonated with me most deeply because of its radical honesty about the human condition. Unlike epics that draw clear lines between heroes and villains, the Mahabharata insists on moral ambiguity. The "good" Pandavas resort to deception to win the war; the "evil" Duryodhana raises legitimate grievances about inheritance. Even Krishna, the divine guide, endorses strategies that bend ethical rules. This complexity mirrors real life, where moral choices are rarely simple and consequences are rarely clean.

I was particularly moved by the figure of Arjuna on the eve of battle — a warrior paralyzed by the knowledge that doing his duty means destroying people he loves. His crisis is universal: we all face moments where our obligations conflict with our compassion, where there is no choice that does not cause pain. The Bhagavad Gita's response — act with integrity according to your dharma, but surrender attachment to results — struck me as one of the most profound pieces of wisdom I have encountered in any literature.

"What is found here may be found elsewhere; what is not found here will not be found elsewhere."

— Vyasa, The Mahabharata

Themes, Characters & Lessons

Dharma vs. Adharma: The epic constantly asks, "What is the right thing to do?" — and refuses to give a single, easy answer. The Cost of War: Victory in the Mahabharata comes at a terrible price. The Pandavas win the kingdom but lose almost everyone they love, demonstrating that war destroys victors and vanquished alike. Karma and Consequence: Every action — noble or ignoble — reverberates through generations. The epic shows how cycles of vengeance and ambition perpetuate suffering. Krishna: As divine counselor, Krishna embodies the paradox of a god who participates in human affairs without resolving their messiness. He guides but does not dictate. Draupadi: One of literature's most powerful female characters, Draupadi challenges patriarchal injustice and demands accountability from the men who failed to protect her dignity. Bhishma: The grandpatriarch bound by his vow, Bhishma illustrates how rigid adherence to personal honor can inadvertently perpetuate systemic injustice.

Personal Connections

Reading the Mahabharata taught me that wisdom does not mean having all the answers — it means having the courage to act thoughtfully in the face of uncertainty. I realized that the conflicts I face in my own life — between loyalty to family and personal ambition, between speaking the truth and keeping the peace — echo the very dilemmas the Pandavas grappled with thousands of years ago. The epic gave me a framework for thinking about these tensions not as problems to solve but as conditions of being human. It showed me that even the greatest heroes are flawed, that victory always comes at a cost, and that the most important battles are the ones we fight within ourselves. The Mahabharata is a text I know I will return to throughout my life, and each time, I expect it will reveal something new.

Part IV

Summary of Learning

Key knowledge, concepts, and skills acquired throughout the Great Books course

Literary Masterpieces Across Eras

Throughout the Great Books course, I gained a rich understanding of literary masterpieces spanning vastly different historical periods, geographies, and cultural traditions. From the ancient Hebrew poetry of Job 38 to the courtly elegance of Heian Japan in The Tale of Genji, from the Sanskrit wisdom of the Panchatantra and the Mahabharata to the Greek heroism of Homer's Iliad and Odyssey, and from the medieval European valor of the Song of Roland — I learned that great literature transcends borders and centuries. Each work offered a unique window into how different civilizations understood justice, honor, love, death, and the divine. I discovered that while cultural contexts vary enormously, the fundamental human questions remain remarkably consistent across all eras: Who am I? What is my duty? How should I face suffering, and what gives life meaning?

Literary Lenses & Approaches

One of the most valuable skills I developed was the ability to analyze literature through multiple critical lenses. I learned that a single text can yield profoundly different — and equally valid — interpretations depending on the analytical framework applied. Reader-Response Criticism taught me that meaning is co-created between text and reader. Moral-Philosophical Criticism revealed the ethical architectures embedded in narrative. Archetypal Criticism uncovered universal patterns of the hero's journey and psychological integration. Formalist/New Criticism showed me how structure, language, and imagery generate meaning independent of authorial intention. Psychoanalytic Criticism exposed the unconscious dimensions of character and narrative. Historical Criticism demonstrated how texts are products of — and interventions in — their cultural moments. And Feminist Criticism illuminated the gender dynamics and power structures that shape literary representation. Together, these lenses gave me a versatile analytical toolkit that I can apply far beyond the study of literature.

Literature as a Mirror of Humanity

Perhaps the most profound lesson of the course was the recognition that literature is not an abstract academic exercise — it is a living record of human experience. The great books we studied are "great" not because they are old or prestigious, but because they continue to speak to the deepest concerns of human existence. The Mahabharata's exploration of dharma is relevant to anyone navigating ethical complexity today. The Odyssey's journey home resonates with anyone who has struggled to find their place in the world. The Song of Roland's meditation on sacrifice speaks to every generation that has grappled with the costs of loyalty. Job 38's unanswered questions about suffering echo in every hospital room, every courtroom, every quiet moment of doubt. I came to appreciate literature as a form of collective memory — a way for humanity to preserve, transmit, and interrogate its accumulated wisdom across millennia.

Critical Thinking & Analytical Skills

The course significantly strengthened my critical thinking, interpretation, and analytical capabilities. I learned to read slowly and deliberately, to resist the temptation of surface-level understanding, and to ask probing questions of every text: What assumptions does this narrative make? Whose voices are included, and whose are silenced? How does the form of the work shape its meaning? What does this text reveal about the society that produced it? I became more comfortable with ambiguity and complexity — recognizing that the best literature often resists easy conclusions and rewards sustained engagement. I also developed stronger writing and communication skills through the process of composing literary analyses: organizing complex ideas, supporting interpretations with textual evidence, and articulating insights with clarity and precision. These skills extend well beyond literary study — they are competencies I will carry into every area of my academic, professional, and personal life.

Part V

Personal Reflection

A meaningful reflection on my overall learning journey in Great Books

1 Understanding & Appreciation of Literature

Before this course, I viewed literature primarily as entertainment — a way to pass the time with compelling stories. Great Books fundamentally changed that perspective. I now understand that every literary masterpiece is a carefully constructed artifact — shaped by its historical context, cultural values, and the author's philosophical commitments. I learned to appreciate not just what a story says, but how it says it: the deliberate choices of structure, imagery, voice, and form that create layers of meaning beneath the surface narrative. Reading has become an active, analytical process rather than a passive one. I no longer ask only "What happened?" but also "Why was it told this way? What does the form reveal? What assumptions does the text make about its reader?" This shift from consumption to critical engagement has made my reading deeper, slower, and infinitely more rewarding. Literature is no longer just stories — it is a conversation across centuries, and I have learned how to participate in it.

2 Challenges & How I Overcame Them

The most significant challenge was the sheer difficulty and cultural distance of many texts. Works like the Mahabharata, the Iliad, and Job 38 were composed in civilizations vastly different from my own, and their narrative conventions, moral frameworks, and literary styles sometimes felt alien and impenetrable. Long verse epics demanded a kind of patience I was not accustomed to as a modern reader. I also struggled initially with the application of literary lenses — it felt unnatural to analyze a text I was still trying to understand on a basic level.

I overcame these challenges through several strategies. First, I learned to approach unfamiliar texts with curiosity rather than frustration, treating confusion as an invitation to dig deeper rather than a reason to give up. Second, I found that re-reading key passages multiple times — each time through a different lens — gradually revealed layers of meaning that were invisible on the first encounter. Third, class discussions and conversations with classmates were invaluable: hearing other perspectives helped me see dimensions of the text I had missed entirely. The challenge of difficulty became, in itself, one of the course's greatest gifts — it taught me that understanding requires effort, and that the effort is always worth it.

3 Most Influential Literary Lens

Of all the critical approaches we studied, Archetypal / Mythological Criticism influenced my interpretation of literature the most profoundly. This lens revealed the deep structural patterns — the hero's journey, the descent into the underworld, the wise mentor, the shadow antagonist, the reluctant hero's transformation — that recur across cultures and centuries. It showed me that humanity has been telling the same essential stories since the beginning of recorded language, and that these recurring archetypes reflect something fundamental about the human psyche and our collective unconscious.

Archetypal Criticism gave me a powerful framework for connecting seemingly unrelated works: I could see the thread linking Arjuna's paralysis on the battlefield of Kurukshetra to Odysseus' perilous journey home to Roland's defiant last stand at Roncevaux Pass. Each hero faces a threshold, undergoes a trial, confronts a shadow, and emerges transformed — or tragically destroyed. This lens made the "great conversation" of literature feel truly unified, revealing that despite the enormous differences in language, culture, and historical context, these masterpieces are all grappling with the same fundamental questions about what it means to be human.

4 Realizations About Life & Society

Several profound realizations emerged from my journey through these great books:

  • Moral complexity is the norm, not the exception. The greatest works we studied — especially the Mahabharata — refuse to offer easy answers to ethical dilemmas. They taught me that real wisdom lies not in certainty but in the willingness to sit with ambiguity, to weigh competing goods and unavoidable harms, and to act thoughtfully even when no choice is perfectly right.
  • Suffering is universal, but how we make meaning from it varies. From Job's unanswered questions to Achilles' rage to Arjuna's despair, every culture has grappled with the reality of pain. What differs is not the fact of suffering but the frameworks — religious, philosophical, mythological — that different civilizations build to understand and endure it.
  • Perspective is everything. The same event can be heroic or tragic, just or unjust, depending on whose eyes we see it through. Feminist Criticism of The Tale of Genji taught me this most powerfully: what the text celebrates as aesthetic refinement can also be read as patriarchal control. This realization has made me a more empathetic and careful thinker.
  • The past is never truly past. The dilemmas, values, and struggles depicted in works composed thousands of years ago remain startlingly relevant. Human nature, at its core, has not changed — we still wrestle with duty, desire, justice, mortality, and the search for meaning.

5 Applying Lessons Beyond the Classroom

The lessons of Great Books are not confined to the classroom — they are tools for living. The critical thinking skills I developed — the ability to analyze complex information, consider multiple perspectives, identify hidden assumptions, and construct well-supported arguments — will serve me in every academic discipline and professional context, from research papers to workplace problem-solving to informed civic participation.

The empathy I gained from inhabiting the perspectives of characters across cultures and centuries will make me a more thoughtful colleague, leader, and human being. Understanding that every person carries a story as complex as any epic poem will shape how I listen, collaborate, and make decisions that affect others.

The appreciation for ambiguity and complexity will help me navigate a world that rarely offers simple answers — in my career, in my relationships, and in my responsibilities as a member of society. I have learned to be comfortable with uncertainty and to resist the allure of oversimplification.

And perhaps most importantly, the sheer joy of reading — of losing myself in a great story and emerging changed — is a gift I will carry for the rest of my life. Great Books did not just teach me about literature; it taught me about what it means to be human, and it gave me a lifelong practice for continuing that education: the simple, profound act of picking up a book and beginning to read.
